# How Doctors Think (2007)

## Introduction

Have you ever wondered about what is happening inside a doctor’s mind when he is making a diagnosis or coming up with a treatment? In this book, *How Doctors Think* (2007), you will discover how a doctor’s thinking process operates, what kind of examples he may make, and how using simple communication tools can help your doctor think better.

### This book is perfect for

- people who want to gain insight into the mind of a doctor.
- healthcare professionals who want to reduce medical thinking errors.

Jerome Goopman holds concurrently the titles of Dina and Raphael Recanati Chair of Medicine at Harvard Medical School, and Chief of Experimental Meldicine at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, with an extensive background in cancer research. ### Lessons in a nutshell

“Mistakes in healthcare can lead to undesirable if not disastrous consequences. Therefore doctors should be making as few mistakes as possible” is the line of thinking most people often hold. Realistically, doctors can make as many mistakes as other professionals, but probably not the type of mistakes that you might think. They are actually more subtle, less threatening mistakes, but when made by many doctors, those mistakes collectively drive down the overall quality of care. With carefully chosen examples of errors doctors can make from each medical speciality, *How Doctors Think* will give you an insider’s look into the forerunners of the healthcare system – doctors.

In this summary, you will learn:

- what kind of errors doctors usually make
- what you can do as a patient to aid your doctor in the diagnosis/treatment process

# Summary

## 1. Intuition vs. analytical thinking: What kind of thinking mode do doctors actually use?

Diseases are complicated. Therefore, you would naturally expect a doctor to mull over all possible factors before making a diagnosis. However, in reality, doctors rely heavily on intuition to diagnose a disease.

In medical schools, students are taught a process of diagnosing including many steps which typically takes around half an hour. Yet, studies have shown that it actually only takes less than twenty seconds for an experienced doctor to come to a conclusion about a diagnosis. Doctors’ decision-making process can happen so rapidly that if we asked them to provide their “reasoning”, as Robert Hamm, an expert in physician’s cognition research would put it, they perhaps wouldn’t know exactly how to explain.

Unlike scientists, when dealing with new patients, doctors often don’t have the luxury of sufficient data to analyze. Coupled with time constraints, doctors are pushed to make decisions quickly based on a limited amount of data, mostly from a patient’s body language and their interaction with the doctor. According to David A. Schön, an MIT cognition scientist, in medicine, this is called *thought-in-action* – the idea that for doctors, thinking and acting are intertwined. As a result, to quickly assess something, doctors make use of *shortcuts*, which will be explored in the following chapters.

Apart from shortcuts, what else belongs to the doctors that could influence their decision? The answer is their feelings, or emotions. A newly minted intern could be so anxious facing a difficult situation that paralyzed his ability to make a decision, like in the case of a patient named Morgan with chest pain.

> “Most errors are mistakes in thinking. And part of what causes these cognitive errors is our inner feelings, feelings we do not readily admit to and often don’t recognize.”

## 2. Primary care: Deciding if a condition requires more consideration.

The most common first stop for somebody who needs medical care is primary care. To get access to more specialized care, patients would need recommendations from primary care physicians, commonly known as *gatekeepers*.

The gatekeepers have to make decisions quickly. For instance, with a large influx of new kids coming into the office every day, general pediatrician McEvoy has to decide as soon as possible which case would demand his attention more, based on the patient’s parents’ reports and clues from observation of that kid.

Having to see a sizable number of patients each day with little allotted time for a patient also contributes to primary care doctors’ increased levels of stress. For general practitioners like Dr. Bigby, they are capped to a limit of fifteen minutes.

However, among medical specialties, primary care doctors are often underpaid compared to their colleagues. One of the reasons is that primary care often only involves simple procedures which insurance doesn’t tend to pay much.

Being first responders to patients’ problems, primary care physicians have to reduce the amount of misdiagnoses as much as possible. This is challenging sometimes because there’s often a mismatch between communication styles between doctors and patients. For example, the difference between how doctors and patients perceive something can become a breeding ground for misdiagnosing. Dr. Delgado was puzzled as to the reason why her elderly patient’s hypothyroidism wasn’t responding to the treatment involving taking some purple-colored pills. It turned out that the patient couldn’t distinguish among various shades of purple and took the wrong kind of pill, which Delgado had failed to take into account.

These factors may lead to less efficient diagnoses and redirection of patients to specialists.

> “Practicing primary care is like trying to find that one distinct face in the passing train.”

## 3. Do specialists have it easier than primary care doctors? It’s complicated.

Compared to primary care doctors, you would think specialists would have a more relaxed time in figuring out what is going on inside a patient’s problem since there’s not many possibilities for the former to choose from. It’s a false comparison, because specialists just have a different set of problems to deal with. Despite how specific certain diseases might be, specialists can still have problems reaching a clinical decision.

First of all, there still exist a lot of rare conditions that evidence-based treatment is unavailable. For children whose hearts’ left sides have half as much the amount of blood flowing through the right side, it’s not necessary to perform a corrective surgery as there are still plenty of children with good prognosis without receiving an invasive procedure. This “fact” doesn’t come from evidence, but from cardiologist Lock’s first-hand experience, doing a clinical trial involving this condition would pose numerous time and ethical constraints.

Second, logic sometimes is incorrect when it comes to complicated conditions with little data, coupled with the difference in biological makeups of each patient. When there’s no sufficient clinical data about a condition, it’s easy to not take into account unknown variables when assessing. Let's continue with Dr. Lock. He had to find out how wrong he was about a kid needing an operator for his heart problem using purely logic after presenting to a national cardiology meeting. Even a tiny change could turn a problem around; in this case, it could prevent a surgery.

If a specialist is feeling uncertain about something, it’ll be guaranteed to be showing. There are a couple of signs to look for:

**Overconfidence:** Doctors tend to display this even though data doesn’t suggest that they should have this high level of confidence regarding a patient’s outcome.

**Bias toward positive information:** Sometimes doctors overtly focus on positive data and neglect negative one.

**Confirmation bias:** The doctor tends to seek out what confirms his beliefs about the patient.

Finally, different specialists can come up with different diagnoses/treatment plans for a single condition. Take an illness like prostate cancer as an example. With that single illness, there are surgeons who prefer to perform surgery, radiation treatment, or chemotherapy treatment.

> “Uncertainty sometimes is essential to success.”

## 4. Emergency care: How to make the most of the minuscule.

Emergency doctors are responsible for giving out diagnoses quickly for patients in the state of emergency with numerous constraints working against them.

In an ER, everything happens at a rapid pace. Thus, emergency doctors don’t have the luxury of having a lot of time in their hand to do complicated thinking about patients’ problems. Quite the contrary, simple thinking sometimes trumps sophisticated mathematically-based decision making when there’s no readily available medical histories.

In the case of a boy named Nathan, before he was correctly diagnosed with leukemia, ER doctor Alter couldn’t use probability theory to come up with a possible diagnosis. However, by being keen with his original observation and not extrapolating, he was proved correct eventually that Nathan’s problem was more serious than it looked.

Similar to other doctors, ER physicians can make a variety of cognitive errors. We begin first with the most common one – *availability error*, also known as *representative error*. This cognitive error happens when doctors judge that if some symptoms are more prevalent or some conditions must be more common than the others. For instance, having all the “bad cold” symptoms available, Begaye’s doctor took quite some time before coming up with the right diagnosis – viral pneumonia.

In misdiagnosing a case of chest pain in the ER, Dr. Croskerry showed that he’d made two cognitive errors:

**Affective error:** When doctors are being biased toward certain patient’s ideal outcomes, they start cherry picking data that support those outcomes.

**Attribution error:** Doctors may come to a conclusion about a patient based only on negative stereotypes.

> "We are diagnosticians, but not comprehensive diagnosticians. Often whatever is bothering a patient, it's flying below the level of our clinical radar.”

## 5. Should you choose the best surgeon available?

Doctors from different specialties can offer different treatment plans for a single condition. It turns out that even within a specialty, doctors may still disagree with one another as well.

A doctor recently started to have some problem with his right hand, so he decided to see some surgeons for consultation. He went to five surgeons in total. Not all surgeons get the right diagnosis, let alone correct treatment. Each hand surgeon has a different way of assessing a medical case and shows (or doesn’t show) certain kinds of cognitive errors which leads to under/overdiagnosis and overtreatment. All the surgeons will be divided into two groups: unsuccessful and successful groups.

For the unsuccessful group, we have three surgeons, all of whom are well-known physicians in their subfield. Unfortunately, none of them made the right diagnosis. The first surgeon – surgeon A has *commission bias*, which drives that surgeon to do anything for the patient regardless whether it’s called for. Surgeon B committed another cognitive error, termed *satisfaction of search*. This cognitive error happens when a doctor is satisfied with something, he’ll stop looking for other hypotheses. Surgeon C, not as subtle as the above two, explicitly refused to admit that there could be a possibility any diagnosis is wrong.

On the contrary, we have a successful group. A young surgeon D who’d just had a few years under his belt was able to avoid the errors the other group made and offered the right diagnosis. What separated him from the other surgeons was not only his willingness to look for additional answers but also his awareness on the limitation of technology. Finally, we have surgeon E, who followed up on the diagnosis of surgeon D and successfully operated on our doctor’s hand. Before the surgery, Dr. E also set reasonable expectations by communicating frankly about recovery rates with the patient.

> “Terry Light had no doubt that with each passing year Dr. D will only get better, because a searching mind guides his hands.”

In the end, those who are best at treating patients may not be the most famous doctors, but those who are willing to think outside of the usual framework.

## 6. What does a radiologist often see when he looks at an X Ray?

For radiologists, imaging technology is the vital tool of the trade. Nevertheless, even when assisted with technology, radiologists can’t be 100% certain about a diagnosis.

To understand why it is so hard for radiologists to come up with a diagnosis, we begin first with radiologist Orwig’s explanation of radiology. Radiology is a two-part process: observing and thinking about the observed. These two parts however cannot be conducted independently, but constantly intertwined with each other and updated when the other part has been changed.

Since thinking is involved in this process, cognitive errors are bound to happen. Each scan has a huge number of images generated, and a radiologist has to comb all over them. Now add on the limited time for each scan, and the number of scans taken each day, the error rates for misdiagnosis go up linearly, as was demonstrated in Potchen’s study. As if radiologists have different sets of eyes, there’s variation in a radiologist’s conclusion on a patient’s problem, which is called *intraobserver variability*. Even more surprising, with *intraobserver variability*, a radiologist can change their mind when looking at the images at a later time!

Similar to our surgeons in chapter 5, radiologists can commit *search satisfaction* and *availability errors* as well. As with the proliferation of technology in many fields, overreliance on man-made machines drives the error rates in making diagnoses up, which is the conclusion of Samei’s summary on various radiological operations. Finally, surprisingly, the difference in the language used by non-radiologists and different radiologists themselves creates a barrier in effective communication.

With a long list of challenges above, what should a radiologist’s response be? Fortunately, Orwig has developed a solution to his own problem, which is to slow down the image classifying process and be more aware of what he’s looking at. For patients worrying about their radiologist not “seeing the right thing”, it’s worth getting a second opinion from a different one.

## 7. Cancer and intensive care: How doctors react to challenging situations.

Oncologists and intensive care doctors, faced with tremendous challenges from treating terminally ill patients, aren’t immune against making errors in thinking.

Shira Stein was placed in the ICU immediately upon landing in Los Angeles. She'd got five virulent microbes all rolled into one disease: severe combined immunodeficiency disorder (SCID). However, Shira, in fact, only had nutritional deficiency, which leads to a form of immunodeficiency; there’s nothing severe like the above diagnosis. In making the wrong diagnoses, the ICU doctors treating Stein had made the following thinking errors:

**Mental prototypes:** Shira symptoms resembled closely those of SCID, which prompted the doctors to agree on this diagnosis.

**Retreating from zebras:** Doctors often shy away from making a rare diagnosis, the rare diagnosis being a “zebra”.

**Diagnosis momentum:** Similar to search satisfaction. In this instance, it’s difficult for a doctor to change the diagnosis or think of other alternative explanations once a diagnosis has been made.

On the side of cancer doctors, in oncologist Nimer’s experience, it’s not that oncologists don’t make the same errors as their colleagues, but the severity of their patients’ problems is more likely to be caused by “emotion” errors. Cancer doctors’ preoccupation with diagnosis algorithms often comes at a patient’s expense, while simultaneously ignoring what makes the patient unique, as each cancer is unique.

On the other hand, the fear of failure and sense of uncertainty is looming over the horizon as there’s no guarantee that a treatment would bring about a benefit the patient’s seeking for. It is more alarming that a lack of communication between cancer doctors and patients can lead to the former’s misunderstanding of the latter’s treatment goals. Why? Because for a cancer patient, surviving is not always the goal. They might want to be able to enjoy life without excruciating pain from invasive procedures, which may only prolong their life for a short amount of time.

To conclude, due to the nature of their conditions, patients have to see ICU doctors and cancer doctors over a long period of time. Therefore, fostering a good relationship with a patient can help doctors avoid cognitive errors associated with diagnosing/treating them and have better understanding of patients’ true wishes.

## 8. The unbreakable relationship among the pharmaceutical industry, insurance companies, and healthcare.

To take a break from an operation room, we step outside of a doctor’s mind to see what else could influence his medical decision, besides his education, knowledge, and his colleagues. Look no further, there’s a direct tie between the pharmaceutical industry, insurance companies, and healthcare. These two forces are shaping every doctor’s decision every day in the world.

Since the number one priority of a pharmaceutical company is profit, those companies employ heavily marketing tricks on both doctors and patients to persuade them to buy their medicine. A non-exhaustive list of marketing tricks can include: giving gifts to doctors, sponsoring trips to conferences, placing ads in medical magazines and journals, creating easy-to-follow surveys that doctors could use to administer drugs, etc. They are what Duggan’s company uses when they tried marketing a testosterone product to reverse aging in men (which research has shown is a non-issue.) Nonetheless, it’s not only money that influences a doctor in endorsing a drug, or a treatment plan, but sometimes doctors can be true believers of a drug despite the lack of evidence supporting its efficacy.

Insurance companies also have a say in what treatment a doctor should issue. To save costs, they tend to only pay for more complex procedures, which gives rise to an increased number of unnecessary procedures being ordered. Case in point: Even though it’s been shown that there’s little benefit for treating chronic back pain with fusion surgery, the amount of this surgery stays largely the same because of how lucrative the payout is and the dependence of many other industries on it.

Concerned with the above external influences, a number of solutions have been proposed, such as more transparent disclosure of conflicts of interest and better patient advocacy, a prime example of which is teaching how a patient could make an informed choice.

In short, doctors should take into account all that are influencing their final medical decision and make sure they are not overdiagnosing/overtreating their patients.

## 9. Go-to questions to ask your doctor to help him think better.

Below is a list of question prompts for patients and their families to ask their doctors to facilitate better communication. By asking the right question, patients can help doctors arrive at a better, more improved assessment/treatment plan.

When you suspect your doctor is misunderstanding you, you can ask: “Can I retell my story with this [medical] problem?”

If your doctor’s decision hasn’t resolved your worry, you could mention it as: “Could my problem be X?”

An extension of the above is to think about an alternative your doctor might have missed: “Can it be another Y problem?”

On the other hand, if you don’t think the diagnosis has encompassed all the symptoms, you can suggest: “Can there be another condition that’s a better fit for my symptoms?”

If you think your doctor hasn’t done enough searching for a correct diagnosis, don’t hesitate to raise your concern: “Can I have multiple problems?”

Lastly, after some course of treatment, you may still endure some symptoms, so you can voice your worry as simply as: “My symptoms haven’t gone all away.”

> "Tell me the story again as if I'd never heard it—what you felt, how it happened, when it happened."

# Conclusion

For doctors, like any other human, striving to avoid making all mistakes is a futile effort. However, both doctors and patients can benefit from better understanding of common errors the former may make. The key here is to “reduce”, not “eradicate”. After being aware of these thinking traps, the next step is effective communication, for without it, no mutual goal could be established and reached.

## Takeaway Lessons

- First impressions matter. Your doctor can make a diagnosis in the span of seconds.
- If you have any doubt about your doctor’s assessment, don’t be afraid to probe him for more information.
- Be aware of common thinking traps a doctor may have and communicate your concern over them with him.
- “Most prestigious” is not a synonym of “the best”: When the quality of care on average is the same among different healthcare providers, choosing a doctor who is a good fit for you trumps another “world-renowned” one who doesn’t know you well.
- Insurance companies tend to not pay for simple procedures, which encourages overtreatment.
